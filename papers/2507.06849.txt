Abstract—Neural network (NN)-based Digital Predistortion
(DPD) improves linearization for wideband radio frequency (RF)
power amplifiers (PAs) but often increases the complexity of
the digital back-end. This paper presentsOpenDPDv2, an open-
source end-to-end framework that unifies PA modeling, NN-DPD
learning, and deployment-oriented optimization.OpenDPDv2
introduces temporal residual (TRes)-Delta Gated Recurrent Unit
(DeltaGRU), a delta-RNN DPD architecture with a lightweight
temporal residual path for robust operation under aggressive
temporal sparsity, and supports joint optimization with fixed-
point quantization. On a 3.5 GHz GaN Doherty PA driven by
a TM3.1a 200 MHz 256-QAM OFDM signal, the FP32 TRes-
DeltaGRU model achieves−59.9dBc Adjacent Channel Power
Ratio (ACPR) and−42.1dB Error Vector Magnitude (EVM).
With 56% temporal sparsity and W12A12 quantization, the
model uses 450 active parameters while maintaining−51.8dBc
ACPR and−35.2dB EVM. Timing-accurateGem5-based evalu-
ation further indicates a 4.5×reduction in forward-pass energy
for this deployment-oriented operating point. Code, datasets,
and documentation are publicly available at https://github.com/
lab-emi/OpenDPD.
Index Terms—digital predistortion (DPD), temporal sparsity,
power amplifier (PA), recurrent neural network (RNN), digital
signal processing (DSP)
I. INTRODUCTION
The exponential growth of wireless data traffic has increased
the demand for wider spectrum use, higher data rates, and
lower error rates in modern communication systems. However,
wideband Radio Frequency (RF) Power Amplifiers (PAs), a
key component of transmitters, introduce inherent distortions
that increase out-of-band emissions and degrade in-band signal
quality, resulting in higher EVM and symbol errors, thereby
reducing both communication reliability and energy efficiency.
To mitigate these effects, Digital Pre-distortion (DPD) is
widely adopted.
DPD seeks to approximate the inverse transfer function
of the Power Amplifier (PA) to pre-compensate nonlinear
distortion before transmission. Conventional V olterra-series-
based DPD methods provide effective linearization in nar-
rowband scenarios, but often fail to meet communication
standards for wideband, high-order modulated signals [1]. In
contrast, Machine Learning (ML) methods, particularly Neural
Network (NN)-based DPD, have been shown to outperform the
Generalized Memory Polynomial (GMP) model for Orthog-
onal Frequency Division Modulation (OFDM) signals above
200 MHz while satisfying standard requirements [2]–[5].
*Corresponding author: Chang Gao
However, the DPD block itself consumes a significant share
of the power in wideband digital radio back-ends [6], and
the use of Neural Networks (NNs) can further aggravate this
issue. Such overhead conflicts with the goal of energy-efficient
transmitters in base stations and Wi-Fi access points, where
power budgets are constrained.
Prior efforts to reduce DPD energy consumption include
lowering the sample rate [7], using a sub-Nyquist feedback
receiver in the observation path [8], dynamically adapting
model cross-terms to the input signal [9], simplifying DPD
computational paths [10], and pruning less important weights
in fully connected layers to induce static spatial sparsity [11].
This work,OpenDPDv2, extends the end-to-end DPD
learning ofOpenDPDv1and the threshold-based recur-
rent sparsity of DeltaDPD [12]–[14] by introducing TRes-
DeltaGRU to decouple output dynamics from hidden-state
sparsity, jointly optimizing sparsity and quantization, and
evaluating processor-level energy with timing-accurateGem5.
The main contributions are:
1)TRes-DeltaGRU: a delta-RNN DPD architecture that
decouples output dynamics from hidden-state spar-
sity.We propose TRes-DeltaGRU, which adds a Tem-
poral Convolutional Networks (TCN)-based temporal
residual path to improve robustness under aggressive
sparsity. For a 200 MHz TM3.1a 256-QAM OFDM sig-
nal on a 3.5 GHz GaN Doherty PA at 41.2 dBm average
output power, TRes-DeltaGRU achieves 72.5% sparsity
with 288 active parameters while delivering measured
performance of -52.0 dBc ACPR and -37.0 dB EVM.
2)OpenDPDv2: a reproducible end-to-end learning
and optimization pipeline.We releaseOpenDPDv2,
a unifiedPyTorch-based framework that (i) trains
DPD models end-to-end through a frozen PA surrogate
and (ii) supports joint optimization with delta-threshold
temporal sparsity and quantization-aware training. For
the TM3.1a 200 MHz test signal,OpenDPDv2supports
TRes-DeltaGRU variants down to 450 active parameters

3)Gem5-based deployment analysis for power-aware

A. Dynamic Temporal Sparsity in DPD Signals and Hidden
States
The delta-processing principle is motivated by the temporal
redundancy of DPD signals. Considering the input feature
set

This temporal redundancy enables dense-matrix-dense-vector

III. OPENDPDV2
To train and optimize the proposed model, we use
OpenDPDv2, a unified end-to-end learning and deployment-
oriented optimization flow shown in Fig. 2. End-to-end train-
ing is realized by cascading the DPD model with a pre-trained,
frozen PA surrogate and optimizing the DPD parameters using
a loss defined at the PA output. In this way, the learned
DPD directly compensates the nonlinear PA behavior in the
cascaded system. This unified flow also makes the comparison
between dense and sparse models more consistent, since the
same training objective, PA surrogate, and evaluation proce-
dure are maintained throughout the optimization process. It
therefore provides a practical basis for studying how temporal
sparsity and fixed-point quantization affect the linearization-
efficiency trade-off within a single reproducible pipeline.
OpenDPDv2contains three stages:
•Data acquisition and PA modeling:collect the baseband
input/output pair(X,Y)and train a behavioral PA model
πP Awith BackPropagation Through Time (BPTT).
•Temporally sparse DPD learning:place
TRes-DeltaGRU before the frozenπ P A and train
the cascade to mapx t toGx t, so that the learned DPD
approximates the inverse PA response.
•Quantization-aware optimization:apply quantization-
aware training [15] using quantized weights and activa-
tions in the forward pass while updating full-precision
variables during backpropagation.
Selected sparse and quantized operating points are exported
toCand evaluated withGem5to connect algorithm-level
compression with processor-level energy behavior.
IV. EXPERIMENTALSETUP
A. Measurement Setup
Figure 3 shows the experimental setup used in this study.
The OFDM baseband I/Q signal was generated by an
R&S-SMW200Asignal generator and amplified by the PA at
the same average output power, with and without DPD. The
output signal was then digitized by anR&S-FSW43spectrum
analyzer. For ACPR measurements, the resolution bandwidth
was set to 100 Hz to lower the analyzer noise floor [16]. The
EVM was computed by comparing the input signal with the
digitized output signal rather than the reference grid.
B. Test Signals and Design-Under-Test (DUT)
The experiment usedAPA_200MHz, aTM3.1a5×40-
MHz (200-MHz) 256-QAM signal with a Peak-to-Average
Power Ratio (PAPR) of 10.01 dB at Complementary Cumu-
lative Distribution Function (CCDF) = 0.001% (9.24 dB at
CCDF = 0.01%) and a sampling rate of 983.04 MHz. The
dataset contained 98 304 samples, split into 60%/20%/20% for
training, validation, and testing. Experiments were conducted
on a 3.5 GHz GaN Doherty PA DUT (internal evaluation board
AR211132 from Ampleon), operating at an average output
power of 41.2 dBm. The PA exhibits a P1dB of 46.5 dBm, a
P3dB of 50 dBm, and a flat gain bandwidth of 200 MHz.

C. Neural Network Training
End-to-end training was implemented onOpenDPDv2.
The pre-trained 2751-parameter DGRU PA behavioral model
achieved -39.96 dB-NMSE. Models were trained offline for
240 epochs using ADAMW with an initial learning rate of
5E-3, aReduceOnPlateauschedule, and a batch size of
64. In the model comparison experiment, the Decomposed
Vector Rotation (DVR) decomposition used 3 units, the Phase
Gated (PG)-Just Another NETwork (JANET) model used a
window size of 4, both JANET models used a hidden size
of 12, and TRes-DeltaGRU used 15 hidden neurons. Each
Conv1D tuple denotes m(C in, Cout, K, D, P): input/output
channels, kernel size, dilation, and padding. Thus, the dilated
layer configuration is(2,3,3,16,16), while the channel mixer
configuration is(3,2,1,1,1). All experiments were run on
a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU with PyTorch 2.4.1 and
CUDA 12.4, and the checkpoint with the best ACPR was used
for final evaluation.

V. RESULTS ANDDISCUSSION
We compare the absolute linearization performance of
OpenDPDv2-trained TRes-DeltaGRU DPD models of differ-
ent complexities against prior work. For an intuitive measure
of sparse-model complexity, the number of active parameters
in TRes-DeltaGRU is defined as
#Active Params=#DeltaGRU Params×(1−Γ)
+#FC Params+#TCN Params (16)
whereΓdenotes the temporal sparsity. In the delta formula-
tion, zero entries in∆ϕand∆hskip the correspondingMAC
operations and associated weight-column accesses; therefore,
the number of active parameters serves as an implementation-
oriented proxy for both active computation and weight-access
activity.
A. Model Comparison
Table I compares TRes-DeltaGRU with five prior DPD
models of about 1000 parameters. It achieves the best over-
all balance, reaching -39.6 dB Normalized Mean Square Er-
ror (NMSE), -42.1 dB (0.79%) EVM, and -59.9 dBc ACPR.
Among the baselines, Block-oriented (BO)-JANET provides
the best EVM and DVR-JANET the best ACPR, but TRes-
DeltaGRU improves on both simultaneously. Quantitatively,
it improves ACPR over BO-JANET by 1.2 dB and improves
EVM over DVR-JANET by 3.5 dB. From an efficiency per-
spective, it is also the smallest model in the comparison
(999 parameters), and its dense FLOPs remain close to those
of TRes-GRU, with only 3.3% overhead. This makes TRes-
DeltaGRU a suitable dense starting point for subsequent
sparsity and quantization optimization.
B. Ablation Study
Table II compares three temporally sparse models:
DeltaGRU without a residual connection, Fully Connected
Residual Delta Gated Recurrent Unit (FCRes-DeltaGRU) with
a linear residual path, and TRes-DeltaGRU with a TCN
residual path. The residual connection clearly mitigates per-
formance loss under temporal sparsity, and the TCN-based
version is best across all operating points. At 0% spar-
sity, TRes-DeltaGRU already achieves the best performance.

C. Sparsity and Quantization Trade-off
Table III summarizes representative operating points from
the joint threshold and precision scans. A key result is that
sparse models can outperform dense ones at lower active
complexity: W16A16 TRes-DeltaGRU-450 improves on dense
FP32 TRes-GRU-524 by 0.5 dB in ACPR and about 2 dB in
EVM while using 14.1% fewer active parameters, and FP32
TRes-DeltaGRU-288 still achieves better EVM than dense
FP32 TRes-GRU-311. This shows that threshold-controlled
temporal sparsity can improve the linearization-complexity
trade-off rather than only degrade performance gracefully.
From a deployment perspective, Table III also exposes several
useful operating regions rather than a single optimum. For
example, the 56.0% sparsity setting preserves strong lin-
earization with only 450 active parameters, while the 72.5%
sparsity setting further reduces active complexity and remains
compatible with the common -45 dBc ACPR target later used
in theGem5-based energy analysis.

999 estimated on theGem5-simulated ARM CPU. W12A12
quantization alone reduces energy by 2.8×versus FP32 while
still achieving -54.5 dBc ACPR, and combining it with 56.0%
and 72.5% sparsity increases the reduction to 4.5×and 5.2×,
respectively, while still meeting a common -45 dBc ACPR
target. These results confirm that the active-parameter reduc-
tion reported in Table III translates into processor-level energy
savings. TheGem5statistics also show that memory access
dominates the forward-pass energy budget: for the evaluated
INT16 cases, L1 D-cache energy is more than an order of
magnitude larger than arithmetic energy, and control overhead
grows with sparsity. This explains why processor-level savings
are smaller than idealized MAC-only estimates, and further
motivates joint algorithm-hardware co-design.

and with TRes-DeltaGRU DPD. Fig. 6(a) shows the corre-
sponding AM/AM and AM/PM characteristics, and Fig. 6(b)
shows the constellation without DPD and with the FP32
TRes-DeltaGRU-999 model.
